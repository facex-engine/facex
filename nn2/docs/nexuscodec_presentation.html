The Problem

Every NVR Company Has a Codec Problem

Video surveillance systems need to decode video from cameras and encode video for storage. Every company in this market relies on third-party codec software — and every option is painful.

FFmpeg — Open Source Trap

Most used video library in the world. Licensed under GPL — meaning any product using it must also be open-sourced. Companies using FFmpeg commercially carry permanent legal risk.

Commercial Licenses — Expensive

MainConcept, Intel Media SDK, and others charge $50,000–100,000/year for codec licenses. Small/medium NVR companies can't afford this.

H.265 Patent Royalties — Triple Tax

H.265 has three separate patent pools (MPEG LA, HEVC Advance, Access Advance). Up to $1.50 per device. Three contracts, three legal processes.

Hardware Lock-in

Intel Quick Sync, NVIDIA NVDEC — tied to specific chips. Can't run on ARM, AMD, or low-cost hardware. No portability.

THE BOTTOM LINE

Thousands of NVR companies worldwide are stuck: use FFmpeg and risk a lawsuit, pay $50K+/year for a license, or spend 2–3 years and $500K+ building their own. There is no good option.

In Simple Terms

What the Decoder and Encoder Do

Decoder = Camera to Screen

IP cameras send video as compressed data (H.265). The decoder converts this back into viewable images in real time.

Without a decoder: the NVR receives data from cameras but can't show anything on screen. It's like receiving a letter in a foreign language with no translator.

Every NVR system needs a decoder. It is not optional.

Encoder = Screen to Storage

The encoder takes video and compresses it for storage on disk or transmission over network.

Without an encoder, you cannot:
• Record video to disk
• Export clips for police/court evidence
• Add analytics overlay (bounding boxes)
• Re-stream to mobile at lower quality

An encoder completes the pipeline.
